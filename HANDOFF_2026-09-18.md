Mechanism, as the data show it: the theatre earns ~20 per unit of labour (ten customers at ~2), a bakery under 7 (two loaves at ~3.4 less grain); with `wage_ceiling_from_own_ask` the theatre always wins the wage auction, and bread cannot pull labour back because the buyer's ceiling (`bread_bid_base_multiplier = 1.3` × trailing price) stops the bread price from signalling scarcity. Hunger appears while bread is still affordable — the report's "price of bread outruns the fixed income" is not what happens; output falls because the workers are at the theatre. Capacity and land do not change the revenue-per-worker ratio, so they do not change the outcome; the ceiling narrows it a little and delays it.

Open: the ratio itself is a modelling choice (one theatre worker serves ten customers; ticket demand has no ceiling like bread's). A run with a less productive theatre, or a ticket ceiling, is the direct test. The wage-ceiling measure (4 loaves) is the existing lever on this ratio and "saves a third" — consistent.

## Code

Default bit-identical. 347 tests. Block `shows` in `all64.jl` (1–4 shows, unlimited, greedy-rich and no-greed at 1 show), SuMSy at 10 seeds (`results/all64_shows_rounds.csv`; the debt arm queued).

## Predictions (written first) and what happened
1. One show (128 seats, ≤ 52 labour units for both theatres): the mixed village survives. **Wrong — 0 of 10, and faster (rounds 28–58).**
2. Collapse returns above some number of shows; 2 borderline, 3 like unlimited. 2 shows: 4 of 10 survive; 3 shows: 0 of 5 so far. Right about where the boundary is, wrong about what is on the other side of it.
3. No-greed village unaffected at 1 show — pending.

## Two ways to die, and the cap chooses between them
At **1 show** every seed dies the same way and it has nothing to do with labour: theatre labour is pinned at 52, bread output sits at the land ceiling (192 = 96 land × 2), the greedy's loaf demand does not all materialise (coin flips), unsold stock knocks the ask down 6 % a round, the bread price falls to ~2.0 against a grain price of 3–4 (per two loaves) plus wages, the bakery's maximum grain bid (expected bread price − ½ wage) drops below the farm's floor, **the grain market freezes, bread output goes from 192 to 0 in one round, and everyone dies three rounds later** — with 96 grain still being harvested and the bakeries holding 100–1,500 in cash. A deflation death: overproduction planned against greedy demand, no cost floor on the ask ratchet, and the grain price locked to a depressed bread price.

At **3 shows and unlimited** the death is the labour drain already described (theatre labour 84–136, bread output collapsing to 14–48 before the freeze). **2 shows** is the crossover: seeds that die show either signature; 4 of 10 avoid both.

So the answer to "if that can save the village, it is a labour-allocation problem tied to capacity numbers" is: capping the theatre does remove the labour drain, and it exposes a second failure underneath. **Prerequisites for a dead village, as the model now states them:** (a) non-essential production can absorb labour at a price essential production cannot match and nobody responds (unlimited or ≥ 3 shows); or (b) the essential good's price is allowed to fall below its input cost and nobody responds (the −6 % ratchet on unsold stock, no floor, grain bid tied to bread price). Both are "no one responds" failures of the price rules, which is the reviewer's §6.2 in both directions. Neither is a property of the fixed income.

Rationing (2 loaves a head first) saved the mixed village in the report; it removes the greedy loaf demand that drives (b) and the ticket spend that drives (a) is then all that is left — consistent.

Deaths from round ~25, 11 alive at 60. **This is the "bread price outruns the fixed income" death the report describes — real here, absent from the norm start.**

Why the two starts differ: the norm start keeps most of the stock in enterprise reserves (demurraged) and persons near their buffer (persons' cash 5.6k of 15k); the equilibrium start hands every person 167 above the buffer (12.7k of 14.7k). Universal, insatiable greed (10 loaves a head; reviewer §6.9) then produces unmet demand in every round, and responsive prices convert permanent excess demand into a permanent 6 %-a-round rise. Responsive prices cure the deflation and labour-drain deaths and create the inflation one; a fixed nominal income is what makes the inflation lethal.

**So the sentence "greed kills only a village whose prices respond to neither scarcity nor loss" (this morning) is wrong as stated.** It holds from the norm start and fails from the equilibrium start. The honest statement: with the money stock at its SuMSy equilibrium, universal mixed greed kills the village whether prices are damped (labour drain / deflation) or responsive (inflation against a fixed GI); greed is survivable only when the greedy are few or the stock is still filling. Which of these is the fair comparison is a judgement call — the equilibrium start is the steady state, but "everyone greedy with 167 to spend" is also the strongest assumption in the model.

# Addendum, 20 September 2026 (late) — government reserve, fiscal policy, clearing

## Code (395 tests)
- **Government reserve** (`government_reserve_in_rounds`, `government_expense_window`, `surplus_redistribution_share`, `surplus_tax_reduction_share`): target = n rounds of expected outlays (trailing mean of public wages, fees, imputed tax on its own wage bill); the surplus above it is paid out per capita and/or returned by lowering taxes. Before this the government hoarded: in the no-greed SuMSy village its cash reached 3,208 — a fifth of the stock — paying demurrage there (it is exempt from the demurrage *tax* only). `manage_government_reserve!` runs after demurrage. Payout rounds down and is capped at cash (a rounding error killed the first run).
- **Fiscal policy** (`tax_policy` :none / :scale / :brackets, `tax_response_coverage`, `tax_response_step`, `bracket_fixed_rise`, `tax_scale_maximum`, `bracket_rate_maximum`): raises on a shortfall (spending above revenue + reserve gap ÷ reserve rounds), sized to cover the coverage share, cuts on a surplus, revenue never moving more than the step per round. `:scale` = one multiplier on every tax (flat wage, capital, demurrage tax, the whole progressive schedule); `:brackets` = each progressive bracket × (1 + r) + fixed points (the 50 % bracket moves five times the 10 % one), capital and demurrage tax follow the scale, the schedule is not scaled twice. # Addendum, 20 September 2026 (night) — why the no-greed village changed with clearing off: a bug

# Addendum, 20 September 2026 — fiscal sweep (debt village, 10 seeds)

**Bug found first**: the fiscal policy's multiplier reached the capital and demurrage taxes but not the flat wage tax withheld at source (`pay_income!` had its own `gross × wage_tax_rate` line) nor `effective_wage_tax_rate`, so the first sweep tripled the scale for a 20 % revenue gain. A test that asserted "the deficit shrinks with the policy on" was replaced by "revenue rises": whether the deficit shrinks is a result, and it turns out to be false.

**Results** (alive of 64 at the end / end round / government debt / tax scale or top bracket / revenue / outlays / wage / bread):
- flat 15 %, reserve rule, no policy: **61.4 / 100 / 14,505** / 1.00 / 72 / 154 / 2.11 / 2.95 — the report's debt village, structural deficit of ~80 a round.
- scale, cover 25 %, 50 % or 100 %, step 2 %: **3.8 / 84 / 7,162** / 3.00 / 44 / 30 / 5.92 / 7.53 — identical across coverage: the shortfall never closes, so the scale climbs at the step every round until its ceiling (45 % wage tax) at round ~60, and the village dies around it.

- brackets, any coverage: **0 / 22**; with +0.5 pt fixed: 0 / 18.

**Reading.** The debt village's deficit is structural — public wages plus a 2-loaf fee against a 15 % tax on a wage bill that cannot bear more. Any rule that raises taxes to close it raises them until the ceiling, and the village dies before the deficit does; with the Belgian schedule it dies without any policy at all. This is `analysis/price_vector.py`'s finding (no feasible price vector at a 30 % wage tax) reproduced dynamically: the wage-tax capacity of this village is somewhere between 15 % and ~25 %, and its public spending needs about twice its revenue. The incremental design works as specified — no step exceeds 2 % — and it is precisely the gradualness that makes the death slow rather than avoided. The downward half of the policy (cuts on surplus) never fires in the debt village because there is never a surplus. The policy is therefore only testable in both directions where a surplus exists: the SuMSy village with the demurrage tax (the `reserve` block showed the cut side working, scale 0.13).

**What this says about the ladder.** Every debt-village rung from 2 on runs a 15 % flat tax and a deficit of 24–45 % of GDP, and now we know why the tax was 15 %: it is roughly the most the village survives. "Keeps everyone alive but runs deficits of 45 % of GDP" (handoff §6) should read "keeps everyone alive *because* it runs deficits of 45 % of GDP: the taxes that would close them kill it". That is a stronger statement for the report than the one it makes.

Not done: a coverage sweep that would find the survivable rate (scale ceiling at 1.3, 1.5, 1.7 instead of 3.0 — `tax_scale_maximum`); the SuMSy side of `:brackets` (no progressive wage tax there, so it is moot).

# Addendum, 20 September 2026 (night) — the weighted mix, and what it revealed about the "best scenario"

## Code
`tax_scaling_weights = (income, consumption, wealth)` replaces `consumption_tax_share`. Three scales; when the policy wants revenue to move by r, family i moves by r × w_i ÷ w̄ (w̄ = revenue-weighted mean weight), each step-limited. Equal weights = one scale (the old rule); 0 = statutory; heavier = more of every change. The wealth tax now has its own scale in the mix. 432 tests. # Addendum, 20 September 2026 (late night) — the six sliders

# Addendum, 20 September 2026 (final) — levers: shift, then move

## Code
`tax_levers = (income, consumption, wealth)` replaces the sliders. Each round the policy first shifts — family i by r × lever_i (positive = with the policy, negative = against, 0 = none) — and then moves every family by r. Total relative move per family = (1 + r·lever)(1 + r) − 1. (0, 0, 0) = one scale; −1 holds a family flat; −2 moves it the opposite way at the policy's rate; the levers turn with the policy, so (−2, +1, 0) relieves wages on a shortfall and cuts consumption first on a surplus. 433 tests. Block `mix` rerun with 14 lever settings; `vat` policy cells, `wealth` best-scenario cells and the missing greed cell queued (`run_lv2.log`, resumable).

# 24 September 2026 — the land market (Stef's decisions)
- `land_pricing = :market` (ladder): one base price (15 months' rent) in both villages; the quote moves 6 % on unserved demand or unsold offers, **never above the most an unserved buyer would pay nor below the least an unsold seller would accept**. Land is worth rent ÷ (levy + the best alternative for the money): debt money — the deposit rate (≈ 400 months' rent for an owner), the bank's rate for a borrowing buyer; SuMSy — idle money loses the parking fee and tax, so without a levy land beats money at any price and nobody sells voluntarily (Gesell).

# 25 September 2026 — land sales at the best bid; valuation from comparable sales
Under `:market` pricing a landowner who must sell (distress) or wants to for reasons of their own (`land_life_event_rate = 0.005` a month) takes the **best bid on the table**: the most any person or farm would pay (its cash reservation) and can pay in cash.
