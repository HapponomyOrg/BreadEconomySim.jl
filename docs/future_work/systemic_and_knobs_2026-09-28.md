# What is systemic and what is a knob — the village on Meadows' ladder of leverage
*28 September 2026. An analysis of the findings of 20–28 September, single runs at 512 villagers unless said. Meadows' list is from "Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System" (Donella Meadows, 1999; twelve places, in increasing order of leverage from 12 to 1).*

## 1. Systemic: what happens whatever the knobs say

These held in every run of the past week — at fixed prices and free, in inflation and in deflation, with the price rules of 20 September and those of today, with and without bailouts, bonds, levies and guarantees.

1. **In the debt village, the money that feeds the idle third has to be borrowed into existence, every month.** The public debt rises at fixed prices (2,500 a month), under inflation (faster), under deflation (slower, but each unit worth more). Bonds change who lends, not that someone must. The only configurations in which the deficit closes are those in which unemployment falls to about 15 % (the productive village), and there the bailouts of failing firms fill the gap the benefit left. The deficit is the cost of unemployment in a money that is someone's debt.
2. **In the SuMSy village the same money is paid as income, and the government owes nothing** in steady state, in every configuration. Its one debt is the startup borrowing while the money stock fills, and it repays that in every run.
3. **The guaranteed income is a nominal anchor.** In every regime the SuMSy village's prices stayed within about ±2 % a year, while the debt village on the same rules ran at +15 % (leaks open), +6 % (step 2), or −5 to −10 % (leaks closed, slack). A fixed sum paid to everyone every month is a floor and a ceiling at once.
4. **Unemployment near a third is structural, not monetary.** It is the same under both moneys, at fixed and free prices: the villagers work for a meal, a cushion and one night out, and once bread takes half the labour there is nothing more they want to make. What the money decides is how the idle are fed, and whether they can be customers — under SuMSy they can, and a service sector employs them (V8: 85 % employment in both villages, but only after the debt village's theatres had failed a hundred times).
5. **In the debt village, firm failure creates money.** A written-off loan leaves the money it created in circulation with no debt behind it; the bank's loss is made good by a government that borrows for it. Every configuration with failing firms showed it (58,500 of bailouts in the "balanced" V8). Under SuMSy nothing of the kind exists: no money is created by lending, so nothing is created by default.
6. **The same deficit has three faces.** Prices held still: a growing debt. Prices free with the leaks open (overpaying, unlimited employer bids, interest booked without earnings): inflation. Prices free with the leaks closed and slack in the labour market: deflation, with the real debt growing. The sign is a matter of knobs; the deficit underneath is not.
7. **Land beats money under SuMSy.** Money that loses value is never a reason to sell land, so land stops trading and its price runs away (Gesell's point), until a levy on land makes it as costly to hold as money; then it trades at the debt village's price. The debt village prices land at the rent capitalised at the deposit rate. Neither depends on the knob values.
8. **A tax on bread kills; a tax on idle money doesn't.** Every fiscal policy that reached bread, in any run, cost lives; the parking tax at any level tried cost none. That is the difference between taxing a flow people cannot avoid and a stock they can spend.
9. **The unemployed under SuMSy are exposed when prices rise**, since the guaranteed income is fixed in money; the debt village's benefit, fixed in loaves, protects them and feeds the inflation. The asymmetry is a rule choice, and it matters in both directions.

## 2. Knobs: what sets the speed or the sign, not the fact

| knob | what it sets | range seen |
|---|---|---|
| how much a buyer will overpay (1.1×, 1.2×, 2×) | the pace of inflation in the debt village | −1 % to +15 % a year at step 2; the same dynamic at 2× as at 1.2×, faster |
| the wage floor (0.5 %, 0.2 %, 0) and how long it outlasts a job | the depth of deflation in slack; the ratchet in tight markets | −9 % to −5 % a year in the productive village |
| the price step (1 %, 6 %) | nothing — the ratchet works through bargaining, not the step | identical results |
| deposit interest, the capital ratio, the bond coupon (0 to 0.375 %) | how much money the banks and the treasury inject | step 8: 25 % → 9 % with interest from equity; the coupon: no effect |
| the owners' guarantee, the write-off delay | where and when a loss lands | totals unchanged: owners have nothing to pay with |
| what a ticket is worth in loaves; how far above the going wage an employer bids | whether a luxury good can set the wage level | step 4: +32 % → −5 % a year |
| the parking fee (2, 3, 4 %) | the money stock each villager settles at, and with it the price level | the guaranteed income buying 38 → 46 % of a month's bread |
| the liquidation delay, the arrears share, the restart threshold | whether a firm dies of a cash-flow hiccup, and comes back | four theatres or one |
| starting prices for a full-money-stock village | whether the unemployed run out of savings before prices settle | 485 → 512 alive |

Meadows' caveat applies to two of these: parameters become leverage points when they push a loop past a threshold. The overpay multiplier at 2× and the theatre's wage bid at its revenue per worker did exactly that — they turned a damped loop into an explosive one — which is why they looked like findings before they were recognised as knobs.

## 3. The village on Meadows' twelve places

**12 — parameters.** Everything in §2. Most of the past week's calibration lived here, and Meadows is right that it rarely changed *what* happened, only how fast. Exceptions as above: the two that reached a loop gain.

**11 — buffers.** The cushion of 30 every villager keeps; the parking-fee-free buffer under SuMSy; the government's reserve target; a firm's working reserve; the banks' 8 % capital. All stabilise. The SuMSy buffer does more: with the fee it fixes the money stock each villager settles at, so it is a buffer that also sets a stock.

**10 — the structure of stocks and flows.** This is where the two villages differ, and the difference is the whole study. Debt money: the stock is filled by loans and drained by repayment and default; every unit is a claim on someone. SuMSy: the stock is filled by a monthly income to everyone and drained by a fee on what sits idle; no unit is a claim on anyone. Findings 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 all follow from this plumbing, not from any parameter. The village's real structure (land, labour, one good and one service) sits here too, and it is what makes unemployment structural (finding 4).

**9 — delays.** Invoices paid a month later; a firm liquidated after three months of arrears; a guarantee written off after six; a worker's floor lasting one month past a job. These set overshoot and oscillation: the liquidation delay decides whether the village churns through firms, the wage-floor memory whether slack turns into deflation. Changeable, cheap, and worth a sweep each.

**8 — negative feedback.** The markets themselves (price up on unmet demand, down on unsold supply); the tax policy against the deficit; the reserve rule against surplus; the capital requirement against lending. The strongest negative loop in the model is SuMSy's own: the more money sits idle, the more the fee removes, so the stock cannot run away. That single loop is why SuMSy prices are bounded in every regime, and it has no counterpart in the debt village, where the only brake on the money stock is repayment, which defaults undo.

**7 — positive feedback.** The debt village runs on them: the wage–price ratchet (cost floor + overpaying + employers bidding what their price recovers); default → debt-free money → bailout → public debt → more money; debt deflation (falling prices → heavier real debt → more failures → more defaults); interest compounding on a stock the interest itself grows; land price → wealth → land; dividends to owners → the rich–poor gap. The knobs of §2 set the gain of the first; the plumbing of place 10 supplies the fuel for the rest. Meadows' advice — slow the loop rather than strengthen the brakes — is what the ticket and wage-bid limits did.

**6 — information flows.** Prices expected from last month's asks; the 5 % thresholds that decide what counts as unmet demand; the land valuation (a quoted price nobody pays, versus the median of what was paid); the transition's reciprocity rule. One missing flow found this week: a market with no sellers shows no signal, so nobody enters an empty theatre market. Another, added: the bailouts and write-offs now stand in every summary table, where they were invisible for a week.

**5 — rules.** Money is a loan or money is an income; loans repaid in euros, not in SuMSy; the benefit in loaves against the guaranteed income in money; limited liability against the owners' guarantee; the protected minimum; Basel; bailouts; taxes by family. The report's clearest single-rule findings sit here: the tax on bread (8) and the transfer's indexation (9).

**4 — self-organisation.** Firms restarted, firms entering, cooperatives forming, villagers choosing their level of participation in the transition, and, later, agents steered by AI. The model has only the beginnings of this, and it is where its next questions lie (the transition is nothing else).

**3 — goals.** The village's goal is that everyone eats; the debt government's is to feed the idle at any debt; the SuMSy government's is the same at no debt; a firm's is to cover its costs, a cooperative's to serve its members. The productive village showed the goal that was missing: a household's standard of living, without which the villagers stop working once bread is cheap.

**2 — paradigm.** Money as a debt, or money as a means. The two villages are the two paradigms made to run side by side under identical rules, and Meadows' own remark applies to what the model is for: you change paradigms by modelling a system, which forces you to see it whole.

**1 — transcending paradigms.** The ladder itself: neither village is the answer; each is a lens, and the honest report keeps both.

## 4. Where the leverage was found

Ranked by what a small change did to the village:
1. **The plumbing of money (10).** No knob moved any result as far as switching from loans to income did: public debt from 300,000 to 0, prices from ±15 % to ±2 %, firm failures from a hundred to none.
2. **The fee-on-idle-money loop (8) and the guaranteed income as anchor (11/10).** One negative loop and one buffer, together the reason SuMSy's price level holds in every regime.
3. **The two rules that turned a loop explosive (12 → 7):** overpaying at 2× and an employer bidding its revenue per worker. Their correction changed the debt village from +32 % a year to −5 % without touching its structure.
4. **The default-and-bailout loop (7/5).** A rule (bailouts) feeding a positive loop; the state stands behind the banks, the banks stand behind failing firms, and the money supply is the residue.
5. **The transfer's indexation (5).** A rule on one line that decides whether the unemployed are protected from inflation or feed it.

Most of what governments argue about — tax rates, interest rates, the coupon, the capital ratio — sits at place 12 and moved little here. That, more than any single number, is what the report can put in front of policy makers.
