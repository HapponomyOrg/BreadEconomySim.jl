Readers who hold that a government's debt in its own currency is no burden will say the twins of §16 settle the matter: let the government borrow the income. The model gives three reasons to hesitate, all of them about incentives rather than law. The debt is not free here: at any positive rate its interest flows to whoever holds it, which in this village means the banks and their owners. A debt that keeps growing is a standing invitation to the policies of §13, and those cost lives. And the twins show what such a policy does once it is applied. Halve the income in the sixth year and nobody dies in either money, though the poorest tenth lose about two thirds of what they hold, in both. Put the debt village under a rule that caps its debt at 60 % of a year's output, the reference value of the euro area's fiscal rules, and the rule cuts the income to ⟦a quarter⟧ as soon as it binds; ⟦twenty⟧ villagers die in the second five years. How much the objection weighs therefore depends on whether a government borrows in a money it issues or in one it does not; under a debt rule, the income is undone by the rule rather than by a vote. An income paid as new money triggers no such rule, because nothing about it is owed.
