# A simulated economy — What it takes to survive
*Talk outline, 25 October 2026. The deck is `deck/what_it_takes_to_survive.pptx`, built by `deck/build.js` (node) from `deck/data.json`. Eleven minutes, eleven slides, one picture each. Goal: curiosity and questions about what a monetary system does, not completeness. Numbers marked (*) are refreshed from the 25 September run before the talk.*

**The thread.** One village, built twice, identical except for where its money comes from. We add one thing at a time and ask the only question a village cares about: who still has bread? Every slide answers that question once and leaves one question open.

---

## 1. Two villages · 0:00–1:00
**Visual:** one village drawn twice, side by side, identical — fields, four bakeries, four theatres, 512 small figures. One word under each: *DEBT* and *SUMSY*. Nothing else.
**Say:** Imagine a village of 512 people. Fields, bakeries, theatres, two banks. Everyone needs two loaves a month; three months without bread, and you die. Now imagine I build it twice, identical, and change one thing: where the money comes from. Then I run each village for ten years, ten times over, and count who still has bread.
**Leave open:** Could that one thing matter?

## 2. Where money comes from · 1:00–2:00
**Visual:** two icons. Left: a hand signing a loan, coins appearing from the signature; a small "IOU" tag on every coin. Right: a calendar with a coin arriving every month for every figure, and a coin slowly melting at the edge of a full purse.
**Say:** In the first village money is what it is for us: it is born when a bank makes a loan, and every coin in your pocket is a coin someone else owes. In the second, everyone receives a fixed income every month — five units — and money that sits idle above a small cushion pays a parking fee: two percent a month simply disappears. Nobody owes anyone for it. That's the whole difference.
**Leave open:** Which village would you want to live in?

## 3. The ladder · 2:00–2:45
**Visual:** a ladder with twelve rungs, each labelled with an icon only: bare fields → government → theatre → bonds → shareholders → cooperatives → reserve → taxes. Both villages climb it.
**Say:** I don't switch everything on at once. Bare fields first. Then a government. A theatre. A stock market. Cooperatives. Taxes. One rung at a time, both villages, same rung, and at every rung: who is alive, what does bread cost, what does the government owe.

## 4. Without a government · 2:45–3:45
**Visual:** the two villages again; the debt village has 14 figures left of 512, the rest faded; the SuMSy village full.
**Say:** Bare fields, no government. In the debt village, fourteen people are left after ten years (*). There is no work for most of them, without work no money, without money no bread. In the SuMSy village everyone lives: five units a month is a meal. Same fields, same bakers. Only the money.
**Leave open:** So where does the debt village find its bread?

## 5. The debt village needs debt · 3:45–4:45
**Visual:** one line chart, ten years: the debt village's public debt climbing to about 190,000 (*); the SuMSy village's line flat at zero. No axes labels beyond "public debt" and "10 years".
**Say:** Add a government: public jobs and a bread allowance for the unemployed. Now everyone lives — and the government borrows about three quarters of what it spends, every month, for ten years. I tried every tax the village could bear. Balance the books, and fourteen people are left. The debt village lives *because* its government goes into debt. And it can never get out: in that village, every coin in circulation is someone's debt.
**Leave open:** Does the SuMSy government never borrow?

## 6. The startup cost · 4:45–5:45
**Visual:** the SuMSy public debt as a small hump: rising for a year and a half, back to zero by year four (*). Beside it the debt village's line still climbing.
**Say:** It does — once. While the new money is still filling up the village, the parking fee brings in little, and the SuMSy government borrows its citizens' savings: about two months of guaranteed income per villager. Then it pays every unit back, in every run (*). A startup cost. One village repays; the other cannot.
**Leave open:** So SuMSy wins? Wait for the next rung.

## 7. Give them shares · 5:45–6:45
**Visual:** the rich–poor gap in meals, before shares, with shares, with co-ops, both villages: SuMSy 23 → 78 → 63, debt 169 → 216 → 187 (*).
**Say:** Now firms get owners, and shares that trade. Money in the SuMSy village had been spread almost evenly. With shares the gap between the richest tenth and the poorest more than triples (*) — though it stays far below the debt village's. Ownership is the other half of the story. Cooperatives take part of that gap back (*).
*(Corrected 25 September: the data does not show the SuMSy gap reaching the debt village's.)*
**Leave open:** What else does money change that we don't think of as money?

## 8. What is land worth? · 6:45–7:45
**Visual:** three plots of land with a price tag each: debt village ~44 months' rent; SuMSy ~1,400 months' rent (*); SuMSy with a land fee and dividend ~49. Tags only, no text.
**Say:** Land. In the debt village land sells for about four years of rent. In the SuMSy village, nobody sells — because money melts and land doesn't, so land becomes the thing to hold, and the rare sale goes for a fortune. Silvio Gesell saw this a century ago. The fix is a fee on land, paid out to everyone as a dividend: then land trades again, at the debt village's price, and the gap between rich and poor is the smallest of all three (*).
**Leave open:** How many other prices does the money decide?

## 9. Make them greedy · 7:45–8:45
**Visual:** a single bakery with a "closed / reopened" sign flipping, and 512 figures all still standing.
**Say:** Then I did something unkind: I made everyone greedy. Spend every surplus, ten loaves at a time, forever. The SuMSy bakeries go bankrupt — and someone reopens one — again, and again, fifty times in ten years (*). And everyone still eats. What it took was two things: prices that move when bread runs short, and a village that starts a new bakery when the last one falls. Money didn't save them. Rules did.
**Leave open:** Which rules do we have?

## 10. What it takes to survive · 8:45–10:00
**Visual:** the two villages once more, now with three small icons above each: (debt) a government that borrows; (SuMSy) a fee that funds the state; both: prices that respond, taxes that don't hit bread.
**Say:** Ten years, ten runs, twelve rungs. What it takes to survive: in the debt village, a government willing to be permanently in debt. In the SuMSy village, an income everyone gets and a fee on idle money that pays for the state — and a fee on land, or the rich end up owning the fields. In both: prices that respond, and taxes that stay off bread — the one tax that killed the village was the tax on bread. And the open questions I can't answer yet: how big a public sector can each money afford (*), and what happens in the ten years when you switch from one money to the other. That's the next simulation.

## 11. Break the village · 10:00–11:00
**Visual:** the village once more, and a QR code. Four words: *The village is public.*
**Say:** Everything here is a rule I chose, and every number comes from a run you can repeat: the code, the village and the report are public. I built one village and broke it a hundred ways. The question I'd leave you with isn't which money is better. It's this: every rule of money is a choice someone made. Which of them would you change?

## Notes for building the slides
- Eleven slides, no bullet lists, at most five words on any slide; the chart slides (5, 6, 7, 8) carry one number each in large type.
- One colour per village throughout (the report's rust for debt money, green for SuMSy); the figures on slide 1 return on 4, 9, 10 and 11.
- Slides 5–8 are the four charts the report already draws from the data; they are rebuilt from the 25 September results.
- Timing is tight at 8 and 10; if the talk runs long, drop the cooperatives sentence on 7 and the public-sector question on 10.
- Questions the audience is being set up to ask: Who decides where money comes from? Why can't a debt village repay? What else does money price? What would a switch look like?
